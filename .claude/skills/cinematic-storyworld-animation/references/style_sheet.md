# Cinematic Storyworld — Reference Video Style Sheet

## Reference inspection summary
The supplied reference is a vertical 65-second video at 720x1280 and 30 FPS. It uses a stylized cinematic story world. Visible locations include a medieval/fantasy throne room, castle interiors, a large armored/ornamental creature or dragon-like subject, crowded castle/courtyard views, castle exterior, sunset garden, moonlit outdoor setting, prison bars, destruction/ruins, an overhead chandelier/ceiling shot, creature close-ups and a final landscape/map-like view.

This document records visual observations, not an asserted plot or character identity.

## 1. Core visual language
- Polished stylized 3D animated-film environments.
- Hybrid character aesthetic: simple graphic facial design (pale round face, minimal dark eyes and mouth) within a dimensional animated body/costume.
- Rich, readable architecture and props.
- Strong foreground/midground/background separation.
- Cinematic camera angles and depth.
- Emotionally legible lighting and color changes.
- Frequent visual changes synchronized to narration.

## 2. Environment design
Use coherent sets rather than generic fantasy backgrounds:
- Throne room: stone arches, columns, stained glass/round windows, banners, red/gold royal textiles, warm firelight.
- Castle hall: stone floor, long perspective lines, chandeliers, wooden furniture, banners and warm practical lights.
- Courtyard/castle exterior: stone walls, towers, gates, flags, crowds and clear daylight.
- Village/garden: cottages, trees, fences, flowers, paths and sunset light.
- Night forest/garden: deep blue moonlight, rim-lit foliage, glowing flowers or practical lights.
- Prison: stone architecture, iron bars, narrow light shafts and restrained color.
- Destruction: collapsed masonry, dust, smoke, embers, damaged rooftops and warm orange atmosphere.
- Overhead architectural insert: ceiling, chandelier or circular structural geometry.
Each environment should have its own visual continuity and consistent landmarks.

## 3. Character language
Main character observed:
- Pale/white circular face.
- Minimal black eyes and mouth, with brows or mouth changes for emotion.
- Simple, readable silhouette.
- Blue robe/tunic-like costume in several shots.
- Face is graphic and simplified; clothing/body and world appear dimensional.
Supporting cast:
- Similar simplified faces.
- Distinct costumes, scale, hair/headwear and silhouettes.
- Maintain the same face language and proportions across the cast.

Do not infer a character's name or historical identity from appearance alone. Use the user's supplied character names or script.

## 4. Large subjects and props
The video uses visually dominant objects and creatures:
- Large grey armored/covered creature or mount with golden dragon-head-like ornaments.
- Close-ups of gold dragon-like heads.
- Castle furniture, throne, banners, tables, cups and chandeliers.
- Prison bars, rubble, city structures and environmental destruction.
Use close-ups and scale contrast to make these objects memorable. If the script identifies the creature or object, use that identity; otherwise describe it neutrally.

## 5. Lighting and color progression
Use lighting to signal emotional shifts:
- Warm amber/gold and red in royal interiors.
- Neutral daylight and soft blue sky for castle exteriors.
- Orange/pink sunset for calm or reflective scenes.
- Deep blue moonlight for night scenes.
- Cool blue/grey for prison or suspense.
- Hot orange/yellow with smoke for destruction.
Keep highlights controlled and preserve readable faces.

## 6. Camera and editing
Observed shot types:
- Medium character framing in the throne room.
- Wide establishing views of halls and castle crowds.
- Tight close-ups of the large creature/dragon details.
- Overhead architectural shot.
- Exterior wide shots.
- Nighttime and prison compositions.
- Destruction and landscape inserts.

Rules:
- Change the visual beat approximately every 1–3 seconds through a cut, camera move, character reaction, prop reveal or lighting/action change.
- Use slow push-ins for reveals, quick punch-ins for jokes or shock, tracking moves for movement, and wide pull-backs for scale.
- Use low angles for imposing subjects and overhead shots for spatial explanation.
- Make cuts motivated by narration.
- Avoid random camera movement or constant zooming.

## 7. Captions
Observed caption treatment:
- White bold sans-serif lettering.
- Thick black outline or shadow.
- Lower-center placement.
- Very short text chunks; the supplied sample often shows one word at a time.
Implementation:
- Add captions in post, not in image-generation prompts.
- Use word-level timing when a transcript/alignment is available; otherwise use short phrase chunks.
- Never cover a face, important prop or action.
- Check safe margins for mobile playback.

## 8. Timing and story rhythm
- Reference duration: about 65 seconds.
- Visual beat changes: roughly 1–3 seconds.
- Use a clear hook, setup, escalation, payoff and ending.
- The scene can remain in one location if the camera/action changes enough to sustain interest.
- Avoid forcing every scene to be a new location.

## 9. What not to copy blindly
- Do not reproduce exact characters, shots, scene sequence, dialogue, logos or proprietary assets from the reference.
- Use it as a visual-language guide and create original scenes for the user's own script.
- Do not make unsupported claims about the plot based only on frames.
- Do not add map overlays unless the story benefits from them.
